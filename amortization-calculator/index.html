What is an amortization schedule?

An amortization schedule is a table that shows every payment on a fixed-rate loan split into interest and principal, with the balance left after each one. The payment stays the same, but its mix changes: interest falls and principal rises until the balance reaches zero on the final payment.

The CFPB describes an amortizing loan (reviewed September 24, 2024) as one where each monthly payment is applied partly to principal and partly to interest. Credit cards do not follow a schedule like this, because the balance and the minimum payment change every month.

The tool charges interest each month at the rate ÷ 12, applies the rest of the payment to principal, and groups every 12 payments into one row, so a 30-year loan fits in 30 lines. The methodology page shows the formula.

The figures cover principal and interest only. Property tax, insurance and lender fees are not included, so a mortgage bill will be higher. For a quick payment figure on any other loan, the loan calculator solves for payment, amount or term.

Why early payments are mostly interest

Early payments are mostly interest because interest is charged on the balance you still owe, and the balance is largest at the start. On a $200,000 loan at 6%, the first month’s interest is $1,000.00 (0.5% of $200,000), so only $199.10 of the $1,199.10 payment reduces the debt.

As the balance shrinks, each month’s interest charge shrinks with it, and more of the same payment goes to principal. The CFPB puts it this way: with a typical fixed-rate loan the combined payment does not change, “but the amounts that go to principal rather than interest do change.”

How extra payments shorten a loan

Extra principal shortens a loan because it lowers the balance that next month’s interest is charged on. In this tool’s example, adding $100 a month to a $200,000, 6%, 30-year loan pays it off in 295 months instead of 360 and cuts total interest from $231,676.38 to $182,537.97.

A few things to check before you send extra money:

  • How it is applied. Ask the lender to put the extra toward principal, not toward next month’s payment.
  • Prepayment penalties. The CFPB notes (reviewed September 11, 2024) that some mortgages charge a fee for paying off early, usually when the whole balance is cleared within the first few years, and that the penalty does not normally apply to small extra principal payments. Your loan documents say which applies.
  • Other debts. Extra money often saves more on a higher-rate balance. The debt payoff calculator tests a lump sum and monthly extras on any single debt.

Results are estimates from the numbers you enter. Your servicer’s statement is the final word.

Frequently asked questions

What is the monthly payment on a $200,000 loan at 6% for 30 years?

The principal and interest payment is $1,199.10 a month. Over 360 payments you would pay $231,676.38 in interest on top of the $200,000 borrowed. Taxes, insurance and any mortgage insurance are extra. A shorter term or a lower rate lowers the total interest; enter both options in the tool to compare them.

Does paying extra principal lower my monthly payment?

Usually not; on a standard fixed-rate loan the required payment stays the same and the loan simply ends sooner. In the example above, $100 extra a month ends a 30-year loan 65 months early. Your monthly budget does not change, but the total interest falls: $49,138.41 less in that example.

Is a 15-year or 30-year schedule cheaper?

At the same rate, the 15-year schedule costs far less interest. At 6% on $200,000, 180 months means $1,687.71 a month and $103,788.46 of interest, against $1,199.10 and $231,676.38 over 360 months. The trade-off is the higher required payment. Enter both terms with your own rate to compare.
