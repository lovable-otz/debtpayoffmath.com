Debt Snowball vs Avalanche

In the debt snowball vs avalanche comparison, the avalanche (highest rate first) usually costs less interest, while the snowball (smallest balance first) closes accounts sooner. On three sample debts the avalanche saved $56.51. Studies in the Journal of Marketing Research (August 2012) and the Journal of Consumer Research (October 2016) tie paying off small balances to people staying with repayment.

What is the difference between snowball and avalanche?

Both methods pay the minimum on every debt and put all spare money on one target. The debt snowball targets the smallest balance; the debt avalanche targets the highest APR. When a debt is paid off, its minimum rolls into the next target, so your total monthly payment never drops until everything is gone.

If your balances and rates line up, smallest balance also carrying the highest rate and so on down the list, both methods pick the same order. In a test of this site’s engine, swapping the sample card rates so the $1,000 card carried 24% made both methods cost exactly $1,038.93.

Side by side: the same three debts

Running the same three debts through both methods, with $200 extra on top of $205 in minimums, both finish in month 27. The snowball costs $1,171.66 in interest and clears its first account in month 5. The avalanche costs $1,115.15, which is $56.51 less, but its first payoff waits until month 11.

What does research say about motivation?

Published research suggests that closing individual accounts helps people keep going. David Gal and Blakeley McShane of Northwestern’s Kellogg School found, in the Journal of Marketing Research (August 2012), that the share of accounts closed predicted whether people eliminated their debt, while the dollar amount closed did not once that share was accounted for.

Their data came from a random sample of 5,943 clients of a debt settlement company who enrolled before January 1, 2007. That matters: these were people in a settlement program, not people paying their own cards, and the study observed outcomes rather than assigning methods at random. A Kellogg Insight article from January 8, 2014 gave an example from the work: someone following a “start small” strategy was about 14% more likely to eliminate the debt after a year, rising to 43% by year four. The same article notes the researchers’ caveat that number and dollar amount of accounts closed are highly correlated, and quotes Gal: “From a rational perspective, you should always pay the higher interest balances first.”

A second line of research points the same way. Keri Kettle, Remi Trudel, Simon Blanchard and Gerald Häubl, writing in the Journal of Consumer Research (October 2016), combined a field study with three experiments and found that concentrating repayments on one account, rather than spreading them, increased motivation to get out of debt. Their explanation is that people judge progress by the largest proportional drop in a balance, which favors small balances. Trudel summarized the work for Harvard Business Review in December 2016.

Neither study measures your own behavior. The math above is certain; the motivation effect is an average across other people.

How to choose

Consider the avalanche if you are confident you will stick with the plan, since it keeps interest lowest. Consider the snowball if seeing an account closed early would help you keep going. When your rates are close together, the cost of choosing the snowball is small, and in the sample above it was $56.51 over 27 months.

A practical way to decide:

  1. Run both. Enter your real balances, APRs and minimums. Each calculator reports the other method’s interest, so the price of motivation is a number, not a guess.
  2. Look at the first payoff month. If the avalanche’s first win is a year or more away, ask yourself whether you would still be paying in month 10.
  3. Check the rate spread. A 24% card next to a 7% loan makes order matter more than two cards at similar rates.
  4. Protect the budget. Both methods need the total payment to stay the same, and new charges on a paid-off card undo the plan.

You can also mix them: clear one very small balance first for an early win, then switch to highest rate.

If a single lower-rate loan could replace several debts, compare that option in the debt consolidation calculator. Results are estimates that charge interest monthly at APR ÷ 12; card issuers charge daily, so statements will differ slightly.

Frequently asked questions

Which is better, snowball or avalanche?

On interest alone, the avalanche usually wins, because every spare dollar goes to the most expensive debt first. The snowball can be better for people who need early wins to keep paying, which is what the Kellogg and Journal of Consumer Research studies point to. Running your own debts through both tools shows what the choice costs you.

Can I switch from snowball to avalanche partway through?

Yes. The methods only decide which debt gets the extra money each month, so you can change the target at any point. To see the effect, enter your current balances in the avalanche calculator and compare its interest figure with the snowball result for the same debts.

What if my smallest debt also has the highest rate?

Then the two methods are the same plan. Both target that debt first, and the order of the rest depends on their balances and rates. When the smallest balance and the highest rate line up across all your debts, the snowball and avalanche give identical payoff dates and interest.
