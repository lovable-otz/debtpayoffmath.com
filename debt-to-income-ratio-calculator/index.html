Debt-to-Income Ratio Calculator

A debt to income ratio calculator divides your monthly debt payments by your gross monthly income, the definition the CFPB uses (reviewed August 28, 2023). With $6,000 of income, a $1,500 housing payment and $650 of other debt payments, your DTI is 35.83%, and your housing-only ratio is 25%.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Debt Payoff Math

How do you calculate debt-to-income ratio?

Add up every monthly debt payment, divide by your gross monthly income (before taxes and deductions), and multiply by 100. That gives your total, or back-end, DTI. Divide your housing payment alone by the same income for the housing, or front-end, ratio. Both use payments, not balances.

The CFPB’s example adds a $1,500 mortgage, a $100 auto loan and $400 of other debts for $2,000 a month. On $6,000 of gross income, that is a 33% DTI.

What counts as debt in DTI?

DTI counts recurring payments on money you owe: your rent or mortgage (with property tax, insurance and HOA dues), car loans, student loans, credit card minimums, personal loans, and court-ordered payments such as child support. Everyday bills like utilities, groceries, phone plans and insurance premiums outside your housing payment are not counted.

Lenders write their own lists. For conventional mortgages, the Fannie Mae Selling Guide (section B3-6-02, April 2, 2025) includes:

  • the housing payment on your home, including the new mortgage,
  • installment debts with more than ten months of payments left, and shorter ones that significantly affect your budget,
  • revolving accounts such as credit cards,
  • lease payments,
  • alimony, child support or maintenance lasting more than ten months, and
  • net losses on rental property.

For credit cards, enter the minimum on your latest statement, not the balance. The credit card payoff calculator shows what that minimum costs over time.

What DTI do lenders look for?

There is no single number. The CFPB says different loan products and lenders set different DTI limits, so the only limit that applies to you is the one your lender uses for that loan. Published limits exist for some loan programs, and the best known are for conventional mortgages.

Fannie Mae’s Selling Guide (B3-6-02, April 2, 2025) sets these maximums for loans it buys:

Fannie Mae underwriting pathMaximum total DTI
Manual underwriting36%
Manual underwriting, meeting its credit score and reserve requirementsUp to 45%
Desktop Underwriter (automated)50%

You may also see 43% cited. That was the DTI cap in the federal General Qualified Mortgage definition. The CFPB’s final rule issued December 10, 2020 removed that 43% limit and replaced it with price-based thresholds.

Other loan types follow their own rules, so ask the lender which ratio it uses. A maximum is a ceiling, not a promise of approval.

How to lower your DTI

You can lower DTI in two ways: reduce your monthly debt payments or increase your documented gross income. Paying off a debt entirely removes its payment from the ratio, which is the most direct lever. Paying down a balance helps only if it lowers the required payment.

Other options to consider:

  • Target the smallest balances first to remove whole payments sooner; the debt snowball calculator shows when each one ends.
  • Refinance or consolidate for a lower payment. This can lower DTI, but a longer term may raise total interest; check it in the debt consolidation calculator.
  • Avoid new debt before applying, since each new payment raises the ratio.

Frequently asked questions

How much of a mortgage can I afford if I make $70,000 a year?

That depends on your lender’s DTI limit and your other debts. $70,000 a year is $5,833.33 a month gross. At Fannie Mae’s 36% manual-underwriting maximum, total debt payments, including the new housing payment, could reach $2,100; at 45%, $2,625; at 50%, $2,916.67. Subtract your other monthly debts to find the room left for housing.

Is 38% a good debt-to-income ratio?

Whether 38% works depends on the lender and loan. For conventional mortgages, Fannie Mae’s Selling Guide sets 36% as the manual-underwriting maximum, allows up to 45% when credit score and reserve requirements are met, and allows up to 50% through its automated system. The CFPB notes that other lenders and products set their own limits.

How can I lower my debt-to-income (DTI) ratio quickly?

The quickest change is usually paying off a small debt in full, because its whole monthly payment leaves the ratio. On $6,000 of monthly income, clearing a card with a $100 minimum lowers DTI by about 1.67 points. Paying down part of a balance may not help unless the required payment drops too.

Can you provide me with a debt-to-income worksheet?

The calculator above works as a worksheet. Enter your gross monthly income, then your housing payment, car loans, student loans, card minimums and other debt payments from your latest statements. It totals the payments and shows both your full DTI and housing-only ratio. Write the numbers down so you can compare them after each debt is paid off.
