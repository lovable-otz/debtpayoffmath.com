Is Debt Consolidation a Good Idea?

Debt consolidation is a good idea when the new loan’s APR, including fees, is lower than what you pay now and you stop adding to the old cards. In this site’s example, an 11.5% loan saves $1,343.58 on $7,000 of card debt. A longer term can cost more overall, the CFPB (reviewed September 2, 2026) warns.

When is debt consolidation a good idea?

Consolidation tends to pay off when three things are true: the new rate, with fees counted, is clearly below your current rates; the new term is not much longer than your current payoff time; and the cleared cards stay paid off. Miss any one of those and the saving can shrink or reverse.

Here is the default case from the debt consolidation calculator. Two cards are replaced by one loan with a 5% origination fee, so the loan must be $7,368.42 for $7,000 to reach the cards.

The new payment is slightly lower, the debt ends six months sooner, and interest plus the fee is $1,343.58 less. That works because 11.5% is far below both card rates, even after the fee.

Getting such a rate depends on your credit. The CFPB (reviewed September 2, 2026) notes that if debt problems have already hurt your credit score, you probably won’t qualify for low rates on a balance transfer, consolidation loan or home equity loan. For context, the Federal Reserve’s G.19 release (September 8, 2026) shows commercial banks averaging 11.86% on 24-month personal loans in the second quarter of 2026.

When does consolidation cost more?

Consolidation costs more when a lower payment comes from a longer term, when fees eat the rate gap, or when the new rate is not much lower. The CFPB (reviewed September 2, 2026) cautions that a smaller payment may mean paying “over a longer time,” which could cost “a lot more overall.” Balance transfers usually carry a fee as well.

The same $7,000 of card debt, run through three loan offers:

The 60-month loan still saves money here, but only $367.85, and it keeps you in debt 18 months longer than your current plan. The 20% loan with a 6% fee costs $786.34 more than simply keeping your current payments.

Other traps the CFPB lists:

  • Promotional rates end. Balance transfer and loan teaser rates can rise later.
  • Late payments. Being more than 60 days late can let a card company raise the rate on all balances, including a transferred one.
  • Your home as collateral. With a home equity loan, missed payments can lead to foreclosure.
  • New spending. Consolidation, in the CFPB’s words, can be “kicking the can down the road.”

Does debt consolidation hurt your credit?

Applying for a consolidation loan or card creates a hard inquiry, and the CFPB says (reviewed September 5, 2025) most credit scoring models look at how recently and how often you apply for credit. Checking your own reports is a soft inquiry and does not affect your scores. This site does not estimate score changes.

What regulators describe beyond that relates mostly to payment behavior:

  • The CFPB (reviewed August 28, 2023) says debt settlement services can have “a negative impact on your credit scores” and your ability to get credit later, partly because these companies often tell you to stop paying your bills.
  • The FTC (December 2025) warns that late payments during a settlement program can hurt your credit.
  • The FTC also notes a debt management plan may require you not to apply for or use more credit until it ends.

The pages cited do not say how long any effect lasts, so this page does not either. Paying the new loan on time and not running the old cards back up are the parts you control.

Consolidation vs debt settlement vs credit counseling

These are three different things. Consolidation replaces debts with new credit that you repay in full. Debt settlement tries to pay creditors less than you owe, with serious risks. Credit counseling reviews your budget and may set up a debt management plan. The CFPB and FTC both warn about settlement.

On settlement, the CFPB warns that penalties and fees on unsettled debts “may wipe out any savings,” that creditors may sue, and that forgiven debt could count as taxable income. The FTC’s March 26, 2026 alert adds that only scammers ask you to pay before they settle any debt or enroll you in a plan, and that anyone who guarantees to settle all your debts or get you fast loan forgiveness is a scammer.

On counseling, the CFPB (reviewed August 2, 2023) says most organizations are nonprofits and suggests getting a specific price quote in writing. Be wary if a counselor pushes a debt management plan as your only option before reviewing your finances.

Frequently asked questions

What are the downsides of debt consolidation?

The main ones are fees, a longer repayment period and the chance of running the old cards back up. Origination and balance transfer fees raise the real cost, promotional rates can end, and a home equity loan puts your house at risk. In this site’s example, a 20% loan with a 6% fee cost $786.34 more than the current plan.

How much is the payment on a $50,000 consolidation loan?

At 11.86%, the Federal Reserve’s average bank rate on 24-month personal loans in the second quarter of 2026, $50,000 costs $1,657.37 a month over 36 months or $1,108.69 over 60 months. If a 5% fee is deducted, you would need to borrow $52,631.58 to clear $50,000, raising those payments to $1,744.60 and $1,167.04.

How to pay off $30,000 in debt in 1 year?

At 22.15%, the Fed’s second-quarter 2026 average for card accounts charged interest, clearing $30,000 in 12 months takes $2,810.00 a month, with $3,719.97 of interest. A lower rate reduces that, which is where consolidation can help if the numbers work. The credit card payoff calculator solves this for your own balance and rate.

How can I consolidate my debt without damaging my credit?

No one can promise that, but the CFPB and FTC pages point to what you control: pay every bill on time, avoid programs that tell you to stop paying creditors, and limit applications, since each one creates a hard inquiry. Checking your own credit reports does not affect your scores.
