What is the debt snowball method?

The debt snowball is a payoff order: you pay the minimum on every debt, then put every spare dollar on the smallest balance, whatever its interest rate. When that debt is gone, its payment moves to the next-smallest balance. The idea is to win early, visible payoffs that keep you going.

Ramsey Solutions, which popularized the method, lays it out as five steps (page updated June 22, 2026):

  1. List debts from smallest to largest balance, ignoring interest rates.
  2. Pay the minimum on every debt except the smallest.
  3. Put as much extra as you can on the smallest debt until it is paid off.
  4. Add that debt’s old payment to the payment on the next-smallest debt.
  5. Repeat until every debt is paid.

The same page argues that personal finance is mostly about behavior, and that quick wins build the motivation to finish. The method is not designed to minimize interest. That job belongs to the debt avalanche calculator, which ranks debts by APR instead. This tool shows you the cost of each order, so you can see what the motivation is worth in dollars.

How does the snowball roll payments forward?

The snowball keeps your total monthly payment the same from start to finish. Your budget is the sum of every minimum plus your extra amount. When a debt is paid off, its minimum does not go back into your pocket; it joins the payment on the next debt, so each target gets paid faster than the one before.

With the default debts, the minimums are $25, $60 and $120. Add $200 extra and your budget is $405 a month, every month.

The rolled-over amounts are approximate within the month a debt closes. In that month, whatever is left of the $405 after the final payment goes straight to the next target, which the calculator tracks to the cent.

The payment on each target grows because the minimums on the other debts were quietly shrinking those balances in the meantime. By month 16, the car loan has already had 15 minimum payments applied.

When does the snowball cost more?

The snowball costs more whenever a small balance has a lower rate than a larger one, because extra money goes to cheaper debt while the expensive debt keeps charging interest. When your smallest debts also carry your highest rates, the snowball and avalanche pick the same order and cost exactly the same.

In the example, Card A (18%) is paid before Card B (24%). That costs $56.51 more than the avalanche, which is small because the balances are modest and the rates are only six points apart. The gap widens when:

  • a large balance carries a much higher rate than the small ones,
  • your extra payment is small, so the expensive debt waits longer, or
  • the balances are bigger overall.

Rate spreads in real budgets can be wide. In the Federal Reserve G.19 release of September 8, 2026, credit cards charging interest averaged 22.15% in the second quarter of 2026, against 7.14% for 60-month new car loans at commercial banks. A car loan is usually last in either order, so the real question is how your cards rank against each other.

Frequently asked questions

How to pay off $30,000 in debt in 1 year?

You need a payment of roughly $2,500 a month plus the interest. If the whole $30,000 were on cards at 22.15%, the Q2 2026 average for cards charging interest (Federal Reserve G.19), clearing it in 12 months would take $2,810.00 a month and cost $3,719.97 in interest. Lower-rate debts need less. Enter your real balances above to see your own figure.

Does Dave Ramsey recommend debt snowball?

Yes. Ramsey Solutions recommends the debt snowball over the avalanche (page updated January 22, 2025). Its stated reason is motivation: paying off the smallest debt first gives a quick win that builds momentum. The same page acknowledges that paying the highest rate first can make more sense mathematically, but argues behavior matters more.

Which is better, snowball or avalanche?

The avalanche costs less interest whenever the two methods pick a different order; with this page’s example debts it saves $56.51. The snowball pays off individual accounts sooner, which some people find easier to stick with. If the two totals are close for your debts, the difference may matter less than which plan you will actually keep following.

How fast can I pay off $20,000 in debt?

It depends on your payment and rate. At 22.15%, $20,000 takes 26 months at $1,000 a month ($5,190.71 in interest) and 74 months at $500 ($16,651.61). Finishing in 12 months takes $1,873.33 a month; in 24 months, $1,039.05. Debts with lower rates clear faster at the same payment.
