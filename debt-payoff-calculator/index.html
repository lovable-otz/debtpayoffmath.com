How much faster will extra payments pay off my debt?

Extra payments speed up payoff because every extra dollar goes to principal, and the balance they remove stops collecting interest. On the tool’s default debt, $15,000 at 9% with a $300 payment, adding $100 a month cuts payoff from 63 months to 45 and saves $1,191.57 in interest.

The effect is largest when the regular payment is small next to the interest. At 9%, the first month’s interest on $15,000 is $112.50, so only $187.50 of a $300 payment reduces the balance. The extra $100 raises that by more than half.

Lump sum or extra monthly payments?

A lump sum paid now saves slightly more than the same money spread over later months, because it removes principal sooner. In the example, $1,000 today saves $574.55; the same $1,000 as $100 extra for ten months saves $512.79. Timing matters more than form: waiting two years to pay it saves only $310.54.

The gap between today and ten months of extras is small, $61.76 here. What decides the result is usually whether the money actually reaches the debt. Monthly extras build a habit and keep cash on hand if something goes wrong. A lump sum, such as a tax refund or bonus, is locked in the moment you pay it.

You can combine both, as the tool’s default does. To see the effect on a longer loan, the amortization schedule calculator shows extra principal year by year.

Should I pay off debt or save first?

Many people do some of each. A CFPB experiment published January 26, 2021 gave 551 participants a hypothetical $5,000 card debt and varying savings; on average they put 50% to 85% of savings toward the debt and kept the rest as a cushion. The right split depends on your rates and how exposed you are to surprise bills.

The case for a cushion is that emergencies otherwise land on credit. The CFPB’s emergency fund guide (October 29, 2025) warns that paying for a one-time emergency with credit can make it “grow significantly larger than your original bill” once interest and fees are added. It suggests basing the goal on what your past unexpected expenses have cost.

The case for paying debt first is the rate gap. Paying down a balance saves its full interest rate with certainty, while savings earn whatever the account pays. The Federal Reserve’s G.19 release (September 8, 2026) shows card accounts that were charged interest averaging 22.15% in the second quarter of 2026.

To weigh the two, consider comparing your debt’s APR with what your savings earn; the interest calculator shows both sides.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my debt payoff date?

Each month, add interest at the APR ÷ 12 to the balance, subtract your payment, and count the months until the balance reaches zero. That is exactly what the tool does. A $15,000 balance at 9% with $300 payments takes 63 months. If the payment does not cover the first month’s interest, the debt never shrinks, and the tool tells you so.

How much do I need to pay to be debt-free in two years?

Enter 24 in the target field and the tool returns the fixed payment. For $15,000 at 9%, it is $685.27 a month, with $1,446.51 of total interest. The target figure ignores the lump sum and extra payment fields, so it answers the question on your current balance alone.

Why does the calculator say my debt will never be paid off?

Your payment is at or below the interest charged each month, so the balance never falls. At 9%, a $15,000 balance collects $112.50 of interest in the first month, so any payment of $112.50 or less leaves you standing still. Raise the payment until the tool shows a payoff date.
