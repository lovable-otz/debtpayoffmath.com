Interest Calculator

$10,000 at 5% for 3 years earns $1,500 in simple interest, or $1,614.72 when interest compounds monthly. This interest calculator works out simple interest and interest compounded yearly, monthly or daily, plus the effective annual rate, the figure federal Truth in Savings rules call APY.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Debt Payoff Math

How do you calculate simple interest?

Simple interest is the principal times the annual rate times the number of years: I = P × r × t. Interest is only ever charged or paid on the original amount, never on earlier interest. $10,000 at 5% for 3 years gives $10,000 × 0.05 × 3 = $1,500, for an ending balance of $11,500.

Most consumer debts do not stay simple for long. This site’s loan tools charge interest monthly on whatever balance remains, and card issuers go further: the CFPB explains (reviewed September 23, 2024) that a daily periodic rate is usually the APR divided by 360 or 365, applied to the balance each day.

How is compound interest different?

Compound interest is charged or paid on the balance including interest already added, so it grows faster than simple interest. The formula is P × (1 + r/n)^(n × t), where n is the number of compounding periods per year.

Here is the tool’s default, $10,000 at 5% for 3 years, under each method:

With yearly compounding, year 1 earns $500, year 2 earns $525 on $10,500, and year 3 earns $551.25 on $11,025. Monthly compounding adds a little interest every month, which lifts the total by $38.47 over yearly. Daily compounding adds only $3.50 more than monthly, so frequency matters less than rate and time.

On debt the same effect runs against you. To see it on a real balance, the credit card payoff calculator and the debt payoff calculator show interest month by month.

What is APY?

APY, or annual percentage yield, is the yearly return once compounding is counted. A 5% rate compounded monthly has an APY of 5.116%; compounded daily, 5.127%. Banks quote APY on deposit accounts, so two accounts with different compounding can be compared on one number.

The tool calculates it as (1 + r/n)^n − 1. The federal Truth in Savings rules express the same idea from the dollars earned: the Regulation DD appendix gives APY = 100 × [(1 + interest ÷ principal)^(365 ÷ days in term) − 1], and says the yield reflects only interest, not bonuses worth $10 or less.

APY and APR are different measures. APY describes what savings earn. APR on a loan describes the yearly cost of borrowing, including fees. The personal loan calculator shows how a fee moves the APR.

Frequently asked questions

How much is 26.99% APR on $3,000?

About $67 of interest in the first month (26.99% ÷ 12 × $3,000), and about $810 over a year if the balance never changed. Cards charge interest daily, so a statement will run slightly higher. Paying $100 a month clears $3,000 in 51 months with about $2,050 of interest, by this site’s monthly estimate.

How do I calculate APR?

APR is the interest rate plus the lender’s fees, stated as a yearly rate. For a loan with an upfront fee, find the rate at which your fixed payments exactly repay the cash you received; the personal loan calculator does this search for you. By law, lenders must show the APR before you sign, so you can check your figure against theirs.

What is 24% APR on a credit card?

It is a 2% monthly rate, or roughly 0.066% a day when the issuer divides by 365 (0.067% if it divides by 360). On a $1,000 balance that is about $20 of interest a month. The Federal Reserve’s G.19 release of September 8, 2026 put the average rate on card accounts assessed interest at 22.15% in the second quarter of 2026.

What is the average APR on a $5,000 loan?

The Fed’s G.19 release does not break rates out by loan amount. Its closest figure is the Federal Reserve’s 11.86% average for 24-month personal loans at commercial banks in the second quarter of 2026. At that rate, $5,000 over 24 months costs $235.04 a month and $640.97 in interest. Your quote will depend on the lender and your credit.
