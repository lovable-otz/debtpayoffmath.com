Debt Consolidation Calculator

A debt consolidation calculator compares what your current debts will cost against one new loan, fee included. With the defaults, moving $7,000 of card debt into an 11.5%, 36-month loan with a 5% origination fee saves $1,343.58 and finishes six months sooner. A bigger fee or longer term can erase that saving.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Debt Payoff Math

How does a debt consolidation loan work?

A debt consolidation loan is a new installment loan that pays off several existing debts, leaving you one fixed payment and one end date. It saves money only if the new loan’s total cost, including fees, is lower than what your current debts would cost, and only if the old accounts stay paid down.

The CFPB (updated September 2, 2026) describes three routes: balance transfer cards, consolidation loans from banks and credit unions, and home equity loans. It flags two traps with loans. Some advertised rates are temporary teaser rates, and a lower monthly payment often means paying over a longer time, which can cost more. A home equity loan adds a bigger risk: if you cannot repay, you could lose your home.

How do origination fees change the math?

An origination fee is usually taken out of the loan before you receive the money, so you must borrow more than you owe to pay everything off. With a 5% fee, clearing $7,000 means borrowing $7,368.42. You pay interest on the fee too, which is why the calculator adds it to the loan’s cost.

The term matters as much as the fee. At a 5% fee, a 24-month loan saves $1,807.56, a 48-month loan $863.63, and a 60-month loan only $367.85, even though its payment drops to $162.05.

When comparing offers, look at the APR, not only the interest rate. The CFPB (reviewed August 28, 2026) describes the APR as the interest rate plus additional lender fees, and the Truth in Lending Act requires lenders to show it before you sign. To see the true APR on a single loan, use the personal loan calculator.

Consolidation loan vs balance transfer card

A consolidation loan gives you a fixed rate, payment and end date. A balance transfer card can offer a lower, often 0%, promotional rate, but only for a limited time, and usually with a transfer fee. The better fit depends on whether you can clear the balance before the promotion ends.

Consolidation loanBalance transfer card
RateFixed for the loan termPromotional rate for a set period, then the card’s regular rate
Upfront costOrigination fee, if anyBalance transfer fee, allowed even on 0% offers
Payoff dateSet by the termNone; minimum payments continue
New purchasesOld cards stay open and can be reusedMay be charged interest right away

The CFPB (reviewed September 23, 2024) says an introductory rate must last at least six months unless you fall more than 60 days behind, and the card must disclose the rate that follows. A separate CFPB answer confirms issuers may charge a transfer fee on a 0% offer. The CFPB’s consolidation page adds that transferred balances lose the grace period on new purchases.

To test a transfer, use the credit card payoff calculator with the promotional rate and a deadline equal to the promotion’s length. For the wider question of whether consolidating fits your situation, read is debt consolidation a good idea.

Frequently asked questions

How much is the payment on a $50,000 consolidation loan?

At 11.86%, the Q2 2026 average rate on 24-month personal loans at commercial banks (Federal Reserve G.19, September 8, 2026), a $50,000 loan costs $2,350.41 a month over 24 months, $1,657.37 over 36 months, or $1,108.69 over 60 months. Total interest runs from $6,409.74 to $16,521.30. An origination fee raises every figure.

How to pay $30,000 debt in one year?

Divide the balance over 12 payments and add the interest. For $30,000 of card debt at 22.15%, that is $2,810.00 a month and $3,719.97 in interest. A lower-rate consolidation loan reduces the interest, but a 12-month loan still needs a payment above $2,500 a month, so the payment size matters more than the rate over one year.

Why does Dave Ramsey say not to consolidate debt?Ramsey Solutions (updated February 28, 2025) gives five reasons: a lower rate is not guaranteed, low rates may not last, lower payments often stretch the term, the debt is moved rather than removed, and spending habits often stay the same. It points people to the debt snowball instead.
How to get rid of $40,000 credit card debt?

Start with a payment you can hold. At 22.15%, $40,000 clears in 36 months at $1,530.72 a month ($15,106.06 interest), or in 26 months at $2,000. The FTC describes nonprofit debt management plans through credit counselors. The CFPB warns that debt settlement can leave you deeper in debt and hurt your credit.
